# Meet the Cast {-}

Three books in, Bellemarsh has learnt to live with seven, then eight, then a very old fox who is
asleep again in the woods. This book is about the first visitor who didn't come to hunt anybody.
He came to find someone.

**Withered Bonnie**: a tall old rabbit with one arm, no face, and two small lights where a face
should be. He stepped out of a tree that shouldn't have opened. He is looking for a friend, and he
will not stop.

**Leo Fontenot and Marisol**: the two people in town who see him first. Leo has learnt, the hard
way, when to put the phone down.

**Agent Okafor**: SCP Foundation, Mobile Task Force Lambda-12. Once, his first summer on the job, a fox
looked him in the eye and decided he wasn't worth the trouble. He still visits the stake he drove
into the ground that night.

**The Eight**: Fredbear and Spring Bonnie still co-lead. Wolf and Freddy still back them up. Chica
still holds the line. Twisted Foxy still runs ahead of everybody, and still visits a clearing she
tells nobody about. Twisted Shadow Bonnie still keeps the Ledger and still keeps a desk at the
Foundation under the name Violet Hellstorm. And **Twisted Bonnie**, the quiet one, the careful one,
is about to meet someone with her shape and her name who has been through far worse than a net.

**The SCP Foundation**: Senior Analyst Rebecca Miller, who has a private file she isn't filing,
and Field Commander Vance, who has never once in her career been told to go to bed by a woman in a
dressing-gown.

**The Global Occult Coalition**: Commander Reyes, who has full command now and a review board that
wishes he didn't.

**Bellemarsh**: Miss Odalys still runs the diner. Fire Chief Delahoussaye still runs the brigade.
And every Christmas, for longer than anyone can remember, the well in front of the Town Hall has
sung, and the whole town has slept through it.

## Act I: Something Interesting {-}

# Chapter 1: The Quietest Week of the Year

Bellemarsh did Christmas the way it did everything else: thoroughly, a little strangely, and
without asking why.

There were lights along Cypress Street, strung from lamp-post to lamp-post in the same scalloped
loops the brigade had hung every December since the posts were gas. There was a tree in front of
the Town Hall, a real one, cut from the county woods and hauled in on the back of the fire truck
with half the town walking behind it. The diner had a paper snowman in the window, though it had
not snowed in Bellemarsh in living memory, and Miss Odalys had been serving gingerbread pancakes
since the first of the month to anyone who asked and several people who hadn't.

And there was the well.

It sat in the middle of the Town Hall square, round and old and ringed with brick gone soft and
green at the base, and every December somebody wound a garland around its little roof and hung a
wreath on its crank. Nobody drew water from it. Nobody had in a hundred years. People threw coins
in it in summer and the council asked them, every year, in a notice nobody read, not to.

Down Cypress Street, in the hour after midnight, the town slept.

Out past the edge of it, in the woods, Twisted Foxy did not.

She went the long way, the way she always did now, out past the bayou roads and the old boundary
line Fire Chief Delahoussaye had walked with his lanterns two months before. Nobody had told her to
visit the clearing. Nobody had asked her not to. She simply went, three or four nights a week, sat
on the same fallen log for a while, and talked to a patch of ground that never talked back.

Tonight the trip had an extra stop on it.

There was a light at the treeline, a small one, the kind of red-filtered torch that people carry
when they don't want to be seen and are about to be seen anyway. Under it, on a folding camp stool
that was much too small for him, a man in a padded Foundation jacket sat with his arms wrapped
around himself, glaring at the woods as if they owed him money.

Foxy knew him. It had taken her a second, but she knew him.

The first summer, the summer of the livestream and the nets and the long, bad night in Midnight
Wood, a very new agent had wandered eleven yards past his marker after what he thought was a
raccoon. She had stood twenty metres from him and looked at him for three long seconds, and decided
he wasn't worth the trouble, and left. He'd driven a stake into the ground on the spot afterwards.
She'd seen it. Most of the pack had seen it. It was still there, a short grey stick half swallowed
by grass, right beside his little stool.

*He came back to his stake,* she thought, *at Christmas, in the cold.* She found she didn't know
what to do with that.

Since Grim's Night, Lambda-12 had put a watch back on this stretch of treeline. It was nothing
serious, one agent a night and a radio check every hour, the sort of watch you keep because your
superiors have to be seen to be doing something. And Agent Okafor, who was not new anymore and
hadn't been for a long time, had asked for the post by the stake. Foxy didn't know that part. If
she had, she would have liked him more.

She went past him at a distance he would never hear, which, for her, was not much of a distance at
all. She sat on her log at the clearing. She told the ground it was nearly Christmas and that the
town had put lights up, and that the agent was back, the one from the first summer, and that he'd
brought a stool.

"You'd have hated him," she said. "You'd have liked him, then you'd have tried to kill him, then
you'd have been sorry about it. That's three feelings. That's more than you had about most
people."

The ground said nothing. It never did.

Foxy went home at four, the long way again. So she wasn't there when the cold got colder, and the
frogs further in stopped singing all at once.

# Chapter 2: Something Interesting

At 2:51 AM, Agent Okafor was explaining to his radio that he was fine.

"I'm fine," he told it. "Hourly check, all quiet, no movement, no raccoons, no anything. It's
Christmas, Delacroix-Ibe. Even the raccoons have gone home."

"Copy, all quiet," said the voice of Lambda-12's second-in-command, from a heated van three miles
away. "You still married to that stake?"

"We're very happy together."

"Copy, very happy. Next check 0351."

He put the radio back on his chest and blew on his hands. He could have had the van. Everybody had
told him so. Vance had looked at him for a long time across her desk when he'd asked for the
treeline post and said, "You know there's nothing out there, Okafor," and he'd said, "Yes, ma'am,"
and she'd signed it anyway. He hadn't been able to explain why he wanted it. He was not sure he
could explain it now. It was just that this was where the world had got bigger, once, and some
nights a man likes to sit where the world got bigger and check it's still that size.

"Bit sad, isn't it?" said someone beside him.

Okafor did not scream. He would remember that later, and be proud of it. He made a noise, but it
was not a scream.

The voice had come from his left, very close, about where his shoulder was. It was a lazy voice. It
sounded like it had been bored for a hundred years and was looking forward to being bored for a
hundred more. And out of the corner of his eye, without turning his head, Okafor could see
something there: a shape like a fox, long-snouted, sitting on its haunches beside his stool, a
shape you could see the trees through. It was greenish, and scorched, and it glowed very faintly,
the way a TV screen glows after you turn it off.

"All on your own, at Christmas," it went on, "sitting next to a stick. I've been watching you for
three nights. You talk to the radio like it's your nan."

"Who," said Okafor, carefully, to the trees in front of him, "is speaking?"

"Nobody. Me. Doesn't matter." It yawned. He heard the yawn. It had far too many teeth in it. "I'm
bored. That's the thing. Nothing's happened round here in weeks, since the big orange one went
back to sleep, and I like it when things happen. So I'm going to do you a favour, stick man,
because I'm in a good mood, and because it'll be funny."

"A favour," Okafor repeated.

"Something interesting is happening tonight," it said, and he could hear it grinning. "Out that
way. Deeper in. You'll want to keep your eyes open."

He did what anyone would have done. He did what every hour of his training had told him never,
ever to do with an unidentified entity at close range. He turned his head and looked straight at
it.

It was right there, its burnt green face a hand's width from his, jaws wide, eyes two white
pinpricks, and it *lunged*. There was a sound with it, a rising electrical shriek like every
smoke alarm in the world going off at once inside his skull, and a flash of green that left a
fox-shaped hole in his vision, and then nothing. No fox. No sound. The stake. The trees. His own
breath, coming very fast.

It took him eleven seconds to find the radio. He counted.

"Lambda-12, Okafor, treeline post," he said. "Requesting — I'd like to make a report."

"Go ahead," said Delacroix-Ibe.

"A see-through fox," Okafor said. "Green. Burnt-looking. It sat next to me and said something
interesting was happening tonight. Then it jumped at my face and vanished."

There was a very long silence on the channel.

"Copy," said a different voice, finally, a voice that should have been asleep. Field Commander
Vance had a radio by her bed. Everyone on Lambda-12 knew that and nobody mentioned it. "Did it say
what?"

"No, ma'am. Just that it was interesting. Deeper in."

Another silence. He could hear her deciding whether he was cold, tired, or right.

"Write it up," she said. "Everything. Exact words. And Okafor?"

"Ma'am?"

"Stay on your stake."

# Chapter 3: 3:33

Ground.

That was the first thing. Ground, soft and wet and covered in leaves, and his foot not knowing what
to do with it. Where he had been, there wasn't any ground. There wasn't much of anything. There was
the blue, and the quiet, and the long, long time.

He stood still and let his foot learn.

Cold. That was the second thing. Cold in the joints, cold in the wires that hung from his elbow. He
couldn't feel the arm that wasn't there, which was how it had been for so long he didn't remember
anything else. He could feel the arm that was there. He moved its fingers, one, two, three, four,
and they creaked, and they moved.

Behind him, the tree flickered once, blue, and went back to being a tree.

He did not look at it. He did not want to see the blue again. He turned his face, which was not a
face, toward the place where the woods thinned out, and he saw lights. Far off, low, warm and
yellow, a lot of them, strung in loops like someone had hung them up on purpose for somebody to
find.

*Town.*

The word came up from somewhere deep, from before the blue. He had known towns. He had known
lights, and music, and children laughing, and a stage with friends on it, standing in a row.

*Friends.*

That word came up too, and it was bigger. It filled him all the way to where his face used to be.
He didn't remember their names. He remembered the shapes of them: a bear, a chicken, a fox with a
hook. And one more. One that didn't stand on the stage with the others. One that lived in a box and
came out to give presents, and sang, and was tall and thin and kind, and had looked after them all,
even at the end, even after the bad thing, after the man whose name—

He stopped. He would not think the name. Something in his chest crackled when he got near it, the
place where his voice lived, the place the man had broken.

*Find them,* he thought instead. *Find them. Find them.*

He went toward the lights. He went clumsily at first, then less clumsily, his long feet crunching
leaves. He went through the dark trees, past a hollow in the ground that smelt of old smoke and
something sleeping, and he gave it a wide berth without knowing why. Then, at the edge of the
woods, he passed a small red light with a man sitting under it.

The man was staring very hard at the trees, the way people stare when they have decided to keep
their eyes open. He was staring, though, in exactly the wrong direction. He was looking deeper in,
where the fox had told him to look. Withered Bonnie passed behind him close enough to touch.

He didn't touch him. He wasn't looking for him.

He went on toward the lights, one foot after another. And somewhere in the broken box in his chest,
a sound came out on its own, a crackle and a hum, the shape of a word he hadn't said in a very long
time.

"Fr— friend."

# Chapter 4: The Duo

"It's the *tail end*," Leo said, for the fourth time. "The Ursids peak on the twenty-second. This
is the twenty-fourth. Technically. It's still worth it."

"It is three-forty in the morning," said Marisol, "and I have seen one meteor, and I think it was a
plane."

They were lying on the bonnet of her brother's car, which was parked, without her brother's
knowledge, at the end of the old logging track where the trees opened up and you could see the
whole sky. School had broken up the day before. Leo's mum thought he was at Marisol's. Marisol's
mum thought she was at Leo's. Both of them had a blanket, a thermos of hot chocolate, and a
growing feeling that this had been a better idea at nine o'clock.

Leo's phone was propped on the dashboard, pointing at the sky. The stream had two viewers. One of
them was Marisol, on her own phone, which she said was "support" and Leo said was "inflating the
numbers".

"Things are different now," Leo said, after a while. "You know? Since October. I look at the woods
and I think, there's stuff in there."

"There *is* stuff in there."

"I know. That's what I'm saying. It's a different feeling, knowing it." He was quiet for a bit.
"It's a better feeling, actually. I didn't expect that."

Marisol was about to say something nice back to him, which she would have regretted, when she saw
it.

It was coming out of the trees on the far side of the logging track, maybe sixty metres away,
walking upright on two long legs. It walked badly, like a person on a boat. It was taller than
either of them, taller than her brother, with two long ears, one bent over at the tip. One arm hung
at its side, thin as a pipe, with something dangling from the elbow. Where the other arm should
have been, there wasn't one.

And it had no face. She could see that even from here, even in the dark. Where the face should
have been there was just a hole, and in the hole, far back, two little lights.

She grabbed Leo's arm so hard he yelped.

"Don't," she whispered. "Don't move. Don't say anything."

Leo looked. She felt him go stiff.

The thing didn't look at them. It walked straight across the logging track, about forty metres
away now, its head turned toward the town the whole time. They could hear its feet in the gravel.
They could hear something else, too, a low crackling hum, like a radio between stations. And
once, as it reached the trees on their side, they heard it make a sound that was almost a word.

Then it was gone into the dark, heading for the lights of Bellemarsh.

Leo's hand had moved. Marisol saw it. It had gone to the phone on the dashboard, the phone that was
already streaming, and his thumb had found the edge of it to tip it down, toward the track. Six
months ago he wouldn't even have thought about it. A year ago he'd have been narrating.

He stopped. His thumb stayed where it was.

Then, slowly, he turned the phone face down on the dashboard and ended the stream.

"Two viewers," he said, in a small voice. "And one of them was you."

"That," said Marisol, "is the most grown-up thing I've ever seen you do."

"Yeah, well." He was shaking. They both were. "Foxy would've killed me. Not *killed* me. You know."

"I know." She was already sliding off the bonnet. "Get in the car."

"Where are we going?"

"Where do you think?" She looked at the dark where the faceless thing had gone, and then the other
way, out toward the bayou and the old cypresses, where the pack slept under the roots. "We tell
them. We tell them *now*."

# Chapter 5: Not on the Ledger

Spring Bonnie had been awake for a long time, and she had been woken for a lot of reasons. A
livestream, once. A net. A strike team. A fox the size of a myth, with a sickle for a hand. She had
been woken for good reasons and bad ones, and over the years she had learnt to tell them apart in
the first two seconds.

Twisted Foxy, sliding down between the cypress roots at 4:05 AM with two out-of-breath teenagers
behind her and her ears flat to her skull, was not a bad reason.

"Tell them," Foxy said. "Tell them what you told me."

They told it. Leo did most of the telling, fast and jumbled and backwards, and Marisol stopped him
every few sentences to put things in the right order. A rabbit. Tall, one arm, no face, two lights
in the hole. Out of the deep woods, walking toward town. A crackling sound. Something that might
have been a word.

The pack listened. All eight of them were there now, folded into the dark under the roots like
furniture under dust sheets. Fredbear sat at the back, enormous and still. Wolf's head had turned
all the way round to watch the kids, which was a thing Wolf did when he was interested and which
Marisol was trying very hard not to look at. Chica was sitting closest, and had, without anyone
noticing her do it, put herself between the two humans and the cold draught from the entrance.

When they had finished, nobody said anything.

Then Spring Bonnie turned to the corner where Shadow Bonnie sat, half there and half not, the way
she always was down here, a dark rabbit-shape with violet eyes.

"Well?"

"He's not on the Ledger," Shadow Bonnie said.

There was a small stir around the roots. Nothing was not on the Ledger. Every human in Bellemarsh
was on it. Every visitor. Every Foundation agent who'd ever set foot in the county, every GOC
soldier, every person who had ever lived in Bellemarsh and done good or harm, back as far as the
pack went. Even Grim Foxy had a page now, though it was a strange one: all observations, no score.

"Nothing?" said Fredbear.

"Not a line. I've never seen him, never heard of him, never had a report of him from anyone, in
all the years I've been keeping it." Shadow Bonnie's eyes narrowed. "He's not from here. He hasn't
been anywhere near here. He's come from somewhere else entirely."

"Like us," said Freddy, from the other side of the roots. He meant it as a joke. It didn't land as
one. The pack had never known where they'd come from either. That was an old ache, and Freddy had
just leant on it.

"No face," said Wolf, quietly. "One arm. Wires. That's not an illusion. That sounds like the real
thing, worn out."

"Like Grim Foxy," Foxy said. "Real all the way through. Cameras would get him."

That landed harder. Everybody remembered October.

"He's not ours," said Shadow Bonnie. "That's the first thing we should be clear about. He isn't
one of us, he isn't on the Ledger, and we don't know what he wants."

"He's *somebody's*," said a quiet voice.

Everyone turned. Twisted Bonnie didn't often speak at pack meetings. She was the infiltrator, the
careful one, the one who listened. Once, the first summer, she had been caught in a Foundation net
and hadn't panicked once. When she spoke, people tended to stop and listen.

"A rabbit," she said. "Old. Falling apart. Walking out of the woods on Christmas Eve toward the
lights, making a noise like a word." She looked at Leo. "What was the word?"

Leo swallowed. "I couldn't— It was all crackly. But I think it was *friend*."

Twisted Bonnie nodded as if he'd said exactly what she'd expected.

"He's not ours," she said again, to Shadow Bonnie, but gently. "But he's looking for somebody. And
you don't walk out of the dark like that, on your own, falling to bits, unless you want to find
them very, very badly."

Spring Bonnie looked at Fredbear. Fredbear looked at the roof of roots, and at the sky beyond it,
which was not yet light but was beginning to think about it.

"Two hours till dawn," he said. "A stranger loose in our town, and every camera in it would see
him. That's the first problem. Whatever he's looking for is the second." He turned his great head.
"Foxy. Bonnie. Find him before the sun comes up. Don't fight him unless he makes you. Get him
somewhere dark."

Foxy was already moving. Twisted Bonnie got up more slowly, unfolding her long limbs one by one.

"And if he doesn't want to come?" Foxy said.

"Then you find out what he does want," said Twisted Bonnie, "and you ask him nicely."

## Act II: Christmas Eve {-}

# Chapter 6: Not Him

The town was full of friends.

That was what he thought at first, walking in under the looping lights at the end of Cypress
Street with the cold in his joints and the crackle in his chest. There were faces everywhere. A
round, smiling face painted on a shop window. A row of little faces in a front garden, glowing
from inside, a snowman and a deer and a man in a red coat. They were looking at him. They were
smiling.

He went to each one. He was very polite about it, the first few times.

The snowman was plastic and hollow and had a light bulb inside it, and it did not know him. The
deer was the same. The man in the red coat, in the garden of a house with its curtains drawn, was
taller and had a beard made of something soft. When Withered Bonnie bent down and put his one hand
on its shoulder and made the crackle that was nearly *friend*, its head turned with a whirr, and
its arm went up and down, and a voice came out of it that said, "Ho! Ho! Ho! Merry Christmas!"

He stood very still.

It was a voice like his used to be, before. Bright and loud and the same every time. Something
inside him lifted, hard and fast, the way your chest lifts when you see someone across a crowd you
think you know.

"Ho! Ho! Ho!" said the man in the red coat. "Merry Christmas!"

It wasn't anybody. It was a box that said the same thing. He knew that after the third time, and
the lift in his chest dropped back down so far it felt like falling. He did not mean to do what he
did next. His hand closed. The man in the red coat's head came off with a sound like a dropped
bucket and rolled under a hedge, still saying "Merry Christmas" in a voice going slowly flat.

A light went on upstairs in the house.

He went. Faster now, less polite. The hardware store on the corner had a Santa in the window too,
a big one, sitting in a sleigh, waving. It was not him either. The glass was between them, and
Withered Bonnie found out what glass was again, all at once, in a long crash that set off a bell
somewhere inside the shop, ringing and ringing.

He kept going. He didn't know where. Down the backs of buildings, where the bins were. Something
smelt warm. There was a light on behind a building with a long window and a paper snowman in it,
and a door at the back propped open with a brick, and steam coming out of it, and a man.

Indaro had opened the diner at 4:45 every morning for over a year now, because Miss Odalys had
asked him to and because it turned out he liked it. He liked the quiet. He liked the coffee
machine warming up, and the radio on low, and the sense that the whole town was still asleep and
he was getting things ready for it. He had his back to the door, cracking eggs into a bowl, when
the light changed behind him.

He turned round. A tall, faceless rabbit with one arm was standing in his kitchen doorway, wires
swinging from its elbow, two small lights deep in the dark where its face should have been.

Indaro had had a complicated life. It had taught him one useful thing, which was that you should
never make any sudden movements in front of anything you didn't understand.

He held up the bowl of eggs.

"We're not open," he said. "Not till six."

The rabbit leant forward. The crackle in its chest rose and fell. Indaro had the feeling it was
looking at him very carefully, for a very particular face, and not finding it.

"Fr—" it said. "Friend?"

"Sure," said Indaro, whose voice was going up at the end of things in a way he couldn't control.
"Sure. Friend. Friends, absolutely, me and you. You want an egg?"

The rabbit made a sound that was all static. It wasn't angry, quite. It was something worse than
angry, something that had hoped and hadn't found. It swung its one arm and the stack of trays by
the door went over with a clatter you could have heard in the next county. Then it was gone,
lurching off down the alley into the dark between the bins.

Indaro stood in the middle of the kitchen with the bowl of eggs and trays all round his feet.

Then he put the eggs down, very carefully, and went to find the phone number Miss Odalys had
written on the inside of the pantry door a year ago. Underneath it, in her handwriting, it just
said: *If anything ever comes in that isn't a customer.*

# Chapter 7: Old Friends

Twisted Bonnie found him by listening.

Foxy found most things by running at them. Wolf found things by smell, Spring Bonnie by hearing,
Shadow Bonnie by already knowing. Twisted Bonnie found things by being very quiet and paying
attention to where the quiet was wrong. There was a smashed shop window on the corner with an
alarm still ringing. There was a garden Santa without a head. There were trays all over the diner
yard, and the light still on. And past the diner, out at the edge of town where the old railway
spur ran into the trees, there was a goods shed nobody had used in forty years. From the shed came
a sound, a thin, crackling hum, going round and round like a song with only one note left in it.

"In there," she said.

Foxy's ears went up. "You sure?"

"Listen to it."

Foxy listened. Then her ears went slowly down again. "Oh," she said. "Oh, that's horrible."

"It's not horrible," said Twisted Bonnie. "It's lonely. It's the same thing from the outside."

The sky over the trees was going grey. Twenty minutes, maybe, before the light was real. They went
in through the gap where the shed's big door had rusted off its runners. Inside it was dark and
smelt of oil and old wood and pigeons. He was in the far corner.

He was sitting on an upturned crate with his back to them, very still, shoulders up round his long
bent ears. The hum was coming from him. When their feet crunched on the grit of the floor it
stopped.

He turned round.

Up close, Twisted Bonnie thought, he was worse than the kids had said, and he was also not worse at
all. The fabric on him had gone to threads and in places to nothing, and you could see the frame
underneath, the struts and joints, old metal gone brown at the edges. The arm he still had was
bare to the shoulder. The other shoulder ended in a socket, and the socket was empty. And his face,
or where his face had been: a dark opening, deep as a well, with two small white lights far back
inside it, steady as stars.

He was the same shape as her. That was the thing she hadn't been ready for. Long and tall and
rabbit-eared, made to stand on a stage. Older. Broken. But the same shape.

The lights in his face went from Foxy to her, and stayed on her.

He stood up so fast the crate went over. His arm came up, fingers spread, and his whole frame
creaked, and the noise from his chest went loud and harsh and wrong, like a radio someone had
kicked. Foxy dropped into a crouch beside her, and Twisted Bonnie put a hand on Foxy's shoulder
without looking at her.

"Don't," she said. "He's frightened."

"*He's* frightened?"

"Of course he is." She didn't move toward him. She didn't move at all. She'd been in a net once,
on a long bad night, and she'd learnt then that the way out of being frightened is for somebody
else to stay very still. "He's somewhere he doesn't know, he's on his own, and two strangers just
walked in on him in the dark. And he's tried to find someone all night, and every face he's found
was the wrong one."

The noise from his chest went up again, then down. The arm stayed up. But it was shaking now.

"My name's Bonnie," she said. "That's Foxy. We're— well. We're a bit like you. You'll have
noticed."

The lights in his face flickered. The arm came down a little.

"Bon—" said the crackle, and stopped, and tried again. "Bon. Nie."

"That's right."

"Bon-nie," he said. He put his hand flat against his own chest, over the broken box. "Bon. Nie."

Foxy said, very quietly, "Oh, no," in the voice of someone whose heart has just been taken out and
handed back to her in pieces.

"You're Bonnie too," said Twisted Bonnie. "I thought you might be."

He nodded, a big clumsy nod, his ears flopping. Then he took a step toward her, and Foxy tensed,
and he stopped, and held the hand up again. Not to grab this time. Open, palm up, the way you
show someone you haven't got anything.

"Look-ing," he said. The words came out one at a time, chewed up by static, like they were being
pulled out of the broken box by hand. "For. My. Friend."

"Which friend?" said Twisted Bonnie, gently. "What do they look like?"

He tried. She watched him try. His hand went up and drew something in the air, long and thin, a
tall narrow shape. He touched his own empty face and drew something on it: two lines going down
from where eyes would be, and a curve for a smile. The crackle in his chest made a shape that
wasn't a word at all. It was, she realised after a moment, a few notes. A tune. Three notes going
up and two coming down, broken and buzzing, but a tune.

Then the static took it, and he made a sound of pure frustration and turned and hit the wall of the
shed, once, hard enough to shake dust down from the roof.

"Okay," said Twisted Bonnie. "Okay. It's okay. We'll help you look."

Foxy turned her head and stared at her.

"We'll help you look," Twisted Bonnie said again, to him, not to Foxy. "But you have to stay here,
in the dark, while it's daytime. People can't see you. If people see you, they'll come with nets,
and you'll never find anybody. Do you understand?"

He looked at the grey light in the gap of the door. He looked at her. And then, slowly, he sat back
down on the floor, because the crate had fallen over, and folded his long legs up, and stayed.

"I'll stay with him," Twisted Bonnie said.

"It's nearly dawn," said Foxy. "You can't be out in the day, not in town. If someone comes in here—"

"Then I'll be very quiet," said Twisted Bonnie, "which is the thing I'm good at. Go back. Tell
Fredbear. Tell the kids to come when they can." She sat down on the floor too, a careful distance
from him, not too close, not too far. "Go on."

Foxy went. At the door she looked back. Two tall rabbit shapes sat in the dark of the old shed,
one of them falling apart and one of them not yet. They weren't looking at each other, just sitting
the way you sit with someone at a hospital.

Foxy ran home faster than she had ever run, and didn't know why.

# Chapter 8: A Phantom Fox, Allegedly

Senior Field Analyst Violet Hellstorm badged in at 7:02 AM on Christmas Eve, said good morning to
the guard by name, asked how his daughter was getting on with the new bike, and found Field
Commander Vance's night log waiting at the top of her pile.

She read it twice. The second time she read it the way she read everything about Bellemarsh, which

*Transparent. Green. Fire-damaged. Vulpine.*

She had never seen it. That was the first thing she noticed, and she didn't like it. For as long as
she had existed, she had made it her business to know every strange thing in that county. She had
heard stories, though. The really old families, the ones who had been in Bellemarsh since before
the bayou roads were roads, sometimes talked about a green thing you saw out of the corner of your
eye, and how you mustn't look at it straight. She'd always filed those away with the stories about
the boundary line and the stories about the well, under *things the town says*.

*Something interesting is happening tonight.*

She put the log down. She thought, very carefully, about the message Foxy had sent through the
shadows an hour before dawn. A stranger, one arm, no face, from nowhere, Bonnie sitting with him in
the railway shed. And now this. A phantom that sat beside an agent and gave him a tip-off.

The phone on her desk rang.

"Hellstorm," she said.

"You've read Vance's log." It was Miller. It was always Miller, these days. Rebecca Miller had got
a corner office after the first summer, and a bigger one after the second, and it had made her no
less likely to ring people up before they'd had their coffee.

"Just now."

"And?"

"And I think Agent Okafor had a cold night on his own in the woods," said Violet, "and a very good
imagination."

"Vance says he hasn't got any imagination at all. She put it in writing." There was the sound of a
pen clicking. Miller clicked her pen when she was thinking, and she was always thinking. "There's
something else. A Bellemarsh resident called the county police at 4:20 this morning to say someone
had decapitated the Santa in her front garden. The hardware store window's smashed. And the diner

"It does," Miller agreed. "Funny how many of them there are."

and it's Christmas Eve, and frankly," she added, "I'd quite like to see what you're like in the
field."

There it was. Light, friendly, said with a smile you could hear down the phone. Miller had a
private file. Violet had known about it since October, the way she knew most things, and Miller
had no idea she knew. It had one name on the front of it.

"Of course," said Violet. "I'd be glad to."

"Wonderful. Car at noon." Click went the pen. "And Violet? Bring a warm coat. Vance says the woods
get cold."

been her dog. It was a very convincing photo.

Then she picked up the night log one more time and read the last line Okafor had reported.

*Out that way. Deeper in.*

*Deeper in than what?* she thought. *And what's out there that I don't know about?*

# Chapter 9: The Price of a Signature

Commander Reyes took the call in his kitchen, in a dressing-gown, with toast going cold on a plate,
because the review board did not believe in Christmas Eve.

"We've had a liaison forward," said Board Liaison Kade, on the screen of his laptop, in the same
pleasant voice Kade used for everything, from good news to the end of the world. "Foundation
night log, Bellemarsh. An apparition at the treeline, a warning about something happening tonight,

"Where my strike team *correctly* didn't act in October," said Reyes.

"That's certainly what the vote said." Kade smiled. Two of the faces in the little boxes behind

days, from before the first summer, when Strike Team 404 had gone into Bellemarsh with nets and

"About a Foundation agent seeing a ghost."

"About a pattern," said one of the two unsmiling faces. "Bellemarsh produces an anomaly. You hold
back. The Foundation holds back. The anomaly goes away on its own, very conveniently, and nobody
ever sees anything. Three times now, Commander. Four, if tonight goes the same way."

Reyes looked at his toast. It was really very cold.

The thing was, they weren't wrong. That was what made it so hard. Bellemarsh *did* keep doing
that. Things *did* keep going away, and he *did* keep holding back, because every time he'd held
back it had turned out to be right, and every time anyone had gone in hard it had gone horribly
wrong. But he couldn't say that to a review board. A review board doesn't want to hear that the
best thing to do about a town is to trust it.

"A team," said the unsmiling face. "Tonight. In Bellemarsh. To capture, not to observe. If
something interesting is happening tonight, we'd like to be the ones holding it in the morning."

Kade raised an eyebrow at him. It was a very small eyebrow, and it said, quite clearly, *I'd take
this one, if I were you*.

"Fine," said Reyes. "A team tonight."

The unsmiling faces looked surprised. Kade did not.

"My terms," Reyes went on. "My people, not yours. Eight, not twenty. Capture gear only, nets and

nobody goes anywhere near a civilian. And if I call it off, it's off, no argument and no appeal,

There was a pause, while the faces in the little boxes looked at each other.

"I know," said Kade, pleasantly. "I haven't got anywhere else to be."

picked up his phone and sent a message to the eight people he trusted most in the world, which
said: *Sorry. Bellemarsh. Tonight. Bring your warm socks.*

And then, because there was no one to see it, he ate the cold toast anyway.

# Chapter 10: He Won't Stay Put

The plan had been simple. Marisol would keep him in the shed. Twisted Bonnie would keep him calm.
Leo would bring supplies, though nobody had worked out what supplies a faceless robot rabbit
needed, so Leo had brought a torch, a blanket, a flask of tea and a packet of custard creams,
on the grounds that you can't go wrong with custard creams.

The plan had lasted until a quarter past ten.

"He's gone," said Marisol, sticking her head back in through the gap in the shed door. "He's *gone*.
I went out for one minute, *one*, to ring my mum—"

"Which way?" said Twisted Bonnie. She was sitting in the darkest corner, folded up so small and so
still that if you didn't know she was there you'd have thought she was a pile of old sacking.

"I don't know! Into town!"

"In daylight," said Leo, faintly. "On Christmas Eve. When *everyone's* in town."

Twisted Bonnie closed her eyes for a second. Then she opened them. "I can't go after him. Not out
there, not in the day. If they see me too—" She stopped. "You'll have to."

"*Us*?"

"He knows you a bit now. You gave him a biscuit." Leo had. Withered Bonnie hadn't eaten it, having
nowhere to put it, but he had held it very carefully for nearly an hour, which Marisol had found so
sad she'd had to go outside. "Find him. Keep people away from him. Bring him back. Please."

They found him in about four minutes, because it turned out you could follow Withered Bonnie
through a small town on Christmas Eve by following the screaming.

It wasn't much screaming, to be fair. Most of Bellemarsh was indoors wrapping presents, and the
bit that was out was mostly too busy to look up. But Mrs Arceneaux, coming out of the post office,
had looked up. And Mr and Mrs Thibodeaux's twins had looked up. They hadn't screamed, they'd waved.
Marisol found that, when she thought about it later, the most worrying part.

He was standing in the churchyard of St. Jude's, in front of the nativity scene.

It was a big one, and old, with painted wooden figures nearly life-sized, and a stable with real
straw. A donkey, a cow, a shepherd, three kings, and in the middle a mother and father kneeling
over a manger. He was standing in front of them with his one hand out, not touching, his head
going from one painted face to the next. Every one of them was looking down at the manger and not
at him.

"Hey," said Marisol, very quietly, coming up on his left side. "Hey. Bonnie. Hi."

The lights in his face turned toward her. The hum in his chest was low and sad.

"Not. Them," he said.

"No," she said. "I know. I'm sorry." She held her hand out, the way you'd hold it out to a dog

# Chapter 11: You'll Want to Be Home Before the Well

Rebecca Miller had been to Bellemarsh four times, and every time it had been a little stranger than
the last, and every time she had been unable to say exactly how.

It wasn't the town. The town was lovely. It was a small, pretty, slightly worn bayou town that
hung its lights on the lamp-posts and its wreaths on the doors and had a diner that would feed you
until you begged for mercy. It was the *feeling* of the town. She had tried to put it in a report
once and ended up writing *it is like being smiled at by somebody who knows something*, which she
had then deleted, because you could not put that in a report.

They got there at half past two. Violet drove. Violet was a very good driver, and very quiet, and
looked out of the window at the woods the whole way in with an expression Miller couldn't read.
Miller had stopped trying to read Violet's expressions a while ago and had started writing them
down instead, in the file nobody else had seen.

They went to the diner first, because everybody went to the diner first.

"Miss Odalys," said Miller, warmly. They had met. Miss Odalys had fed her three times and asked
after her mother twice, and Miller had never told her she had a mother.

"Analyst Miller," said Miss Odalys, in a voice like a door held open. "And Analyst Hellstorm.
Merry Christmas. Sit, sit. You'll have the gingerbread."

They had the gingerbread. Miller asked about the window at the hardware store, and the Santa with
no head, and the trays in the diner yard. Miss Odalys said it was a terrible thing, kids probably,
and Indaro said it was a raccoon, and Miss Odalys said, "Indaro," and Indaro said, "A big raccoon,"
and went back into the kitchen.

Miller wrote *big raccoon* in her notebook and underlined it twice.

They spent the afternoon on Cypress Street, walking. Miller talked to people. She was good at it.
She was friendly and interested and asked about the lights and the tree and what people were
doing for Christmas, and she listened to the answers. She learnt that the Arceneaux family were
having a ham this year instead of a turkey. She learnt that the church had a new animatronic in the
nativity, which was very modern, and did the arm move? She learnt that the brigade had brought the
Town Hall tree in on the back of the fire truck, like always. And she learnt, gradually and then
all at once, something else.

"You'll be heading back before dark, I expect," said the man at the hardware store, sweeping up
glass. "You'll want to be home before the well."

"Before the well?" said Miller.

"Before the well starts." He said it the way you'd say *before the rain starts*. "It's Christmas
Eve."

She asked him what he meant. He looked at her as if she'd asked what Christmas was, and said,
"The *well*, love," and then a customer came in and he went to serve them.

She heard it again an hour later from a woman in the post office. *Get yourselves home before the
well, now.* And again from the twins outside the church, who told her, very seriously, that you
weren't allowed to look out of the window when the well was singing, and that if you did the
lights would see you. Their mother hushed them and laughed and said they were being silly, and
Merry Christmas, and *you'll want to be home before the well*.

By the fourth time, Miller had stopped writing in her notebook and was just holding it.

"Violet," she said, in the car, as the sun went down red over the cypress trees. "What's the well?"

"There's an old well in front of the Town Hall," said Violet. "It's on the heritage list. It's dry.
It's been dry for a hundred years."

"They say it sings."

"Small towns have a lot of folk tales," Violet said. She was looking out of the window again.

"Do you know what it sings?"

"Analyst Miller, I have no idea."

Miller looked at her for a long moment. Violet didn't look back.

Then Miller did something she hadn't done in the whole of her career, which was to ring Field
Commander Vance, on Christmas Eve, and ask her to bring Lambda-12 into town for the night.

"Because of a folk tale," said Vance.

"Because of a folk tale," Miller agreed. "And a phantom fox. And a headless Santa. And because
every single person in this town has told me, today, nicely, that I'll want to be home before
something happens tonight, and not one of them will tell me what. In my experience," she said,
"a monster is not the frightening thing, Commander. The frightening thing is a whole town that
isn't frightened."

There was a pause.

"We'll be there by eleven," said Vance. "I'll bring Okafor. He'll be unbearable about it."

# Chapter 12: Who He's Looking For

Shadow Bonnie had been keeping the Ledger for longer than anyone in Bellemarsh had been alive. She
had seen every Christmas there had ever been in this town.

That was the thing she kept coming back to, sitting in the back booth of the diner at half past
nine that night, in her Violet Hellstorm coat, while Miller went to wash her hands. She had *seen*
every Christmas. And she had heard, every single year, somewhere between midnight and six, the
well in front of the Town Hall begin to sing.

She had never once gone down it.

She had never asked why. It had never seemed like a thing that needed a why. It was one of the
town's things, like the boundary line and the bell and the green shape you weren't supposed to
look at. It wasn't on the Ledger, because it never did anybody any harm. It sang, the orbs came
out of the woods, and in the morning it was Christmas. Every year she had sat somewhere dark and
listened to it, and felt something she'd never had a name for, and gone on with her work.

Exactly like the town, she realised. Exactly like everybody else. All those years
spent telling herself the people of Bellemarsh were the ones who never asked questions, and she'd
been doing exactly the same thing.

Miss Odalys came over with the coffee pot. She filled Violet's cup without asking, the way she
always did, and then she didn't leave. She stood there, holding the pot, looking out of the window
at the dark square at the end of Cypress Street, where the Town Hall tree was lit up and the well
sat beside it in its garland.

"You'll be staying for the song, I expect," Miss Odalys said. "This year."

Violet looked up at her.

"I always wonder," said Miss Odalys, as if they were talking about the weather, "who it is, down
there. Singing to. Every year, all on their own. My grandmother said it was waiting for somebody.
She said there's a kind of song you only sing when you want somebody to hear it from a long way
off." She put the coffee pot down on the table. "Not that I'd know. It's only a folk tale."

She went back behind the counter.

Shadow Bonnie sat very still. Then she slid out of the booth, left money on the table, and stepped
into the shadow by the coat rack. She came out the other side forty yards away in the dark of the
railway shed, which was not a thing Violet Hellstorm could do but was a thing Shadow Bonnie had
done every day for a very long time.

They were all there. Twisted Bonnie, sitting on the floor next to him. Leo and Marisol, wrapped in
blankets on a pile of pallets. Foxy, pacing. Fredbear's huge shape filling the doorway, and Spring
Bonnie's beside it, and the others in the dark outside. It was ten o'clock, and the pack shouldn't
have been out yet, and they were out anyway.

"The well," said Shadow Bonnie, without saying hello. "The one in front of the Town Hall. Every
Christmas, between midnight and six, something down there sings."

The hum in the corner stopped dead.

Withered Bonnie lifted his head. The lights in his face swung round and fixed on her. It was the
first time he had looked at her at all.

"Sing," he said.

"Yes."

"Sing-ing." He got up. Twisted Bonnie got up with him, one hand on his arm, and he didn't seem to
notice it was there. "Sing-ing. Her. Them. The. Song." The static in his chest rose up and up, and
then out of it, broken and buzzing but certain, came the same five notes he had made that morning
in the shed. Three up, two down.

Shadow Bonnie had heard those five notes every Christmas for as long as she had existed. She had
never known they had a beginning anywhere else.

"Oh," said Twisted Bonnie, very softly. "Oh, it's them. It's your friend. They've been down there
all this time."

He was already moving. It took Fredbear, Freddy and Twisted Bonnie together to stop him going
straight out of the shed door and down the middle of Cypress Street. He didn't fight them. He just
kept walking into them, like a tide, his one hand out toward the town.

"Not yet," Twisted Bonnie was saying, into the place where his ear joined his head. "Not yet. Not
yet. Listen to me. There are people out there tonight. People with nets. If you go now, you'll
never reach it. Wait for the song. Wait, and we'll take you. I promise. I *promise*."

He stopped walking. The tide went out. He stood in the middle of the shed with three Twisted
holding him, shaking all over, the five notes going round and round in his chest.

"Prom-ise," he said.

"Promise," said Twisted Bonnie.

Fredbear looked down at both of them for a long time.

"Then it seems," he said, "that we are going to the well."
